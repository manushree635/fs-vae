Abstract

Latent diffusion models (LDM) rely on VAEs to compress visual inputs for computational efficiency, which is particularly consequential for very high resolution visual inputs. Through an in-depth interpretability analysis, we show that this compression disproportionately degrades high-frequency information at lower spatial resolutions, discarding fine details such as facial features or text patterns.

Building on this insight, we propose a cross-resolution finetuning framework we call FS-VAE that uses a higher-resolution VAE as a teacher to supervise its lower-resolution counterpart, recovering lost frequency information via a band-wise steerable-pyramid objective. FS-VAE enables richer reconstruction from low-resolution latents without expanding channel dimensions, reference encoders, external models, or retraining downstream diffusion architectures.

Furthermore, we analyze the impact of distinct loss objectives on reconstruction quality and demonstrate our approach's generalizability across diverse image and video VAEs. By preserving the original architecture and latent dimensionality, FS-VAE acts as an efficient, plug-and-play replacement for existing LDM pipelines.

Investigation: What Low-Resolution VAEs Lose

Steerable pyramid decomposition: Encoding images at 256² rather than 512² leads to a notable loss of high-frequency detail during VAE reconstruction. Because raw RGB representations cannot isolate how and where these fine-grained structural details are lost, we analyze frequency degradation using the steerable pyramid transform. The steerable pyramid is an invertible, shift-invariant multi-scale operator P that decomposes an image into spatial frequency subbands and orientations.

Setup: To conduct our diagnostic study, we apply steerable pyramid decompositions to 512² ground-truth images (IG), their corresponding VAE reconstructions at 512² (ŷT, the teacher signal), and 256² VAE reconstructions upsampled to 512² (ŷS, the student signal). Each image is decomposed into a residual high-pass band H, S = 5 octave-spaced frequency levels (L0–L4, centered at 128, 64, 32, 16, and 8 cycles per image), and a residual low-pass band Lo (< 6 cpi). Each intermediate level is split across O = 4 orientations. Because the steerable pyramid transform is fully invertible, an image can be reconstructed even when a subset of its decomposed frequency subbands is replaced with subbands from another image. We leverage this and systematically swap subbands from the teacher ŷT or ground-truth image IG into the student reconstruction ŷS. Following each swap, we quantify identity preservation using the TokBench metric (F-Sim), defined as the cosine similarity between face-recognition embeddings of ỹS and IG.

This intervention is conceptually analogous to activation patching in interpretability literature, which has been widely applied to LLMs and recently extended to text-to-image and video models. To our knowledge, this is the first interpretability-driven causal analysis to localize frequency-band loss in low-resolution VAE reconstructions.

Frequency Band Swap Test: We decompose each image with a complex steerable pyramid into a highpass residual H, five oriented levels L0–L4, and a lowpass residual Lo. Then replace an individual frequency band in the student reconstruction (ŷS), from either the teacher reconstruction (ŷT), or ground truth IG, leaving the other bands untouched (blue frames). In this example, just swapping the L1 band increases the absolute F-Sim by 0.43. Repeating this for every band over 1000 images from the TokBench, we localize where in the spectrum low-resolution VAEs lag.

Average F-Sim gains on 1000 face images from TokBench obtained from swapping bands between the original, higher-resolution image (IG) or its corresponding VAE reconstruction (ŷT) and the lower-resolution counterpart (ŷS). Notice how mid- to higher frequency bands such as L1 and L2 are the bands where signal is lost in a lower-resolution, 4-channel latent VAEs, while L0 and L1 is where most information is lost in low-resolution 16-channel VAEs.

Findings: Where is face identity lost? We observe several key insights. First, swapping directly from ground truth IG consistently yields higher F-Sim gains than swapping from ŷT, revealing that high-frequency identity information is degraded even in 512² teacher reconstructions. Second, for 4-channel VAEs like SD-VAE (ft-MSE) and SDXL, performance gains concentrate heavily in the mid-to-fine subbands L1 and L2. For example, swapping L1 from ŷT raises F-Sim by +0.147 (+0.208 from IG), while L2 yields gains of +0.118 (+0.132 from IG). In contrast, L0 and L3 contribute only ∼ +0.031, while H, L4, and Lo provide negligible improvements—confirming that information loss at 256² resides primarily in mid-to-fine frequencies. Third, 16-channel VAEs such as SD3.5 and FLUX.1 exhibit a similar profile, however, with more finer bands (L0 and L1). Their gains are smaller due to their higher latent capacity.

Takeaways from Interpretability Analysis of VAEs

  1. The loss of information between 256² and 512² reconstructions can be precisely localized in the frequency domain.
  2. Most information is lost in the mid- to high-frequency bands of low-resolution VAEs.
  3. The loss of high-frequency information is more pronounced in lower-capacity VAEs.

Method

Overview of FS-VAE: The teacher (top) is a frozen VAE which takes a higher-resolution image and its reconstruction is the target for our frequency loss. The student (bottom) encodes the same image at a lower resolution with the same frozen encoder, and only its decoder is trained. Steerable Pyramid operator is applied on both the student and teacher reconstructions and the difference between their magnitude maps is penalized.

Stage 1: Self-distillation in the frequency bands

Our goal is to transfer high-frequency details from a high-resolution teacher to a lower-resolution student via self-distillation. However, standard VAE fine-tuning relies heavily on spatial pixel losses such as MSE or ℓ1, which treat all frequencies uniformly and fail to enforce targeted recovery of specific frequency bands. Thus, we introduce a frequency- and orientation-aware distillation loss computed directly on the steerable pyramid decompositions of the teacher and student reconstructions.

We extract each subband k's magnitude map of ŷT, denoted as aTk = |Pk(ŷT)| and of student's reconstruction ŷS, denoted as aSk = |Pk(ŷS)|, where |·| is a complex modulus operator. These magnitude maps isolate spatial energy across different frequencies and serve as core components for our distillation loss (\(\mathcal{L}_{ ext{dist}}\)). For each sub-band k, we take the pixel wise error on the magnitude maps between the teacher and student, normalized by the teacher's energy in that band, where ⟨·⟩ denotes spatial mean. The loss is the weighted mean over bands:

\[ r_k = \frac{\langle |a^T_k - a^S_k| \rangle}{\langle a^T_k \rangle + \varepsilon}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} = \frac{\sum_k \omega_k r_k}{\sum_k \omega_k} \]

\[ \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell_1} + \lambda_{\text{lp}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{LPIPS}} + \lambda_{\text{dist}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} \]

rk is formulated as a relative metric because coefficient values in low-frequency bands are significantly larger than those in high-frequency bands, which would otherwise cause low frequencies to dominate the gradient. The normalization ensures critical bands are not underrepresented in the optimization objective. Both the teacher and the student models share the same pretrained autoencoder, differing only in the resolution of the input image. Consequently, their respective latents maintain identical channel counts and downsampling factors, differing only in spatial resolutions. To account for the resolution mismatch between the teacher and student outputs, we upsample ŷS via bicubic interpolation to match the teacher's resolution.

Why fine-tune only the decoder? Freezing the encoder preserves the underlying latent representation, prevents latent space drift, and eliminates the need to retrain downstream diffusion models bound to the original latent space. Restricting fine-tuning to the decoder also allows effective adaptation to domain-specific datasets without degrading cross-domain generalizability.

Stage 2: Adversarial post-tuning

(a) High-frequency artifacts that Stage 1 introduces in 4 channel VAEs. The GAN loss removes the artifact and enhances the texture. (b) Power spectrum of the residual: We compute residual maps by subtracting the ground truth and reconstructed images, then applying Fast Fourier Transform on them. We calculate the power at each spatial frequency averaged over 200 images. Notice the bright spots after Stage 1 of training, which are eliminated after Stage 2 of training.

While Stage 1 successfully recovers the missing high frequency details, we empirically observe that in lower capacity (i.e., 4-channel) VAEs, \(\mathcal{L}_{ ext{dist}}\) introduces a subtle periodic grain artifact across the reconstructed image. To diagnose the origin of this pattern, we analyze the 2D Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of the reconstruction residual. Compared to the baseline, the Stage 1 residual magnitude spectrum reveals prominent, localized bright spots at higher frequency regions. We hypothesize that this behavior arises particularly in low-capacity VAEs because forcing the decoder to match the teacher's high-resolution energy profile constrains the total power per frequency band, but does not impose any constraint on where within the band that energy should be allocated.

To eliminate these artifacts, we briefly post-tune the decoder using a compound objective comprising the standard ℓ1 reconstruction loss, LPIPS perceptual loss, and a PatchGAN adversarial loss. The PatchGAN term, particularly, enforces realistic local texture statistics by penalizing non-natural spectral distributions, effectively suppressing high-norm spectral spikes and restoring clean spatial reconstructions.

Results

By matching the per-band energy of the 512² teacher, Stage 1 restores high-frequency details missing from the 256² baseline. This leads to clear improvements in PSNR, SSIM, and F-Sim (+4.12% from 0.45 → 0.47 on TokBench). In Stage 2, the adversarial loss introduces a standard perception–distortion tradeoff, which leads to a slight dip in the pixel-level quality metrics, with an improvement of rFID.

Performance Across Face Sizes

We evaluate FS-VAE across face scales s = max(w, h)/256, defined following TokBench as the longer bounding box side relative to the cropped image dimension. Substantial gains occur on small faces, i.e., for s < 0.2: +8.7% and +10.2%. These improvements taper off as face scale increases, demonstrating that while the baseline effectively reconstructs prominent faces, FS-VAE successfully addresses its weakness on small-scale facial features.

For video models, we apply only the Stage 1 objective to the decoder of the Wan 2.1 VAE. On TokBench the finetuned decoder stays on par with the teacher, with PSNR (30.55 → 30.61) and F-Sim slightly improved. To show that the objective generalizes beyond a single decoder, we also apply it on top of RefDecoder, a reference-conditioned Wan 2.1 VAE decoder, by adding the steerable pyramid term to its original training loss. We compare against RefDecoder fine-tuned on the same data with its original loss only, so the pyramid term is the only difference between the two models. With the pyramid term, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, rFID and rFVD all improve on TokBench (rFID 9.70 → 9.11, rFVD 9.65 → 8.11), and F-Sim increases by 2.73% over the released RefDecoder.

User study

To fully understand the subtle, fine-grained details that FS-VAE is explicitly designed to restore, we conduct a user study. Three participants performed pairwise preference evaluations on 50 image pairs from LAION-Face and TokBench. Reconstructions from the baseline and FS-VAE were displayed side-by-side in a randomized order and position, with human subjects selecting the image exhibiting more realistic fine detail. FS-VAE was preferred in 77% of overall comparisons, receiving 88% preference on LAION-Face and 67% on TokBench.

Compatibility with Diffusion Models

Our key advantage of FS-VAE is its compatibility with many diffusion models built on the SD-VAE latent space. This is primarily because our encoder is frozen and only the decoder is finetuned with the frequency steering loss, thereby a drop-in replacement in the existing pipelines. We show the results on DiT-XL/2, sampling with 250 DDPM steps and learned variance, a classifier-free guidance scale of 1.5, and a fixed seed.

Compatibility with diffusion models: Generations from DiT-XL/2 decoded with the baseline decoder and with FS-VAE. Since the encoder is frozen, both decode the same sampled latents, so any improvement comes from the decoder alone. FS-VAE recovers noticeably more facial detail, particularly around the eyes, mouth, and nose.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrate that information loss in low-resolution VAEs is non-uniformly distributed across the frequency spectrum. In particular, 4-channel VAEs exhibit localized degradation primarily within mid-to-high frequency steerable pyramid subbands. Leveraging this insight, we design a frequency-targeted self-distillation loss that transfers high-frequency details from a high-resolution teacher model. By fine-tuning solely the decoder, FS-VAE preserves full compatibility with downstream diffusion pipelines. Empirically, FS-VAE significantly enhances facial identity preservation across varying scale ranges, particularly on small faces, where conventional VAEs systematically fail. Furthermore, FS-VAE achieves lower FVD scores across VideoVAEs, demonstrating enhanced temporal consistency and identity stability over time.
